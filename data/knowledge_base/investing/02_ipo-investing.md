## What Is an IPO?

An Initial Public Offering (IPO) is the first time a private company sells its
shares to the general public on a stock exchange. After the IPO, the shares are
listed and trade freely on NSE or BSE.

**Why companies do IPOs:**
- Raise capital for growth, expansion, or acquisitions
- Provide an exit for early investors (founders, VCs, PE funds)
- Gain a market-based valuation
- Enhance brand credibility

---

## Types of IPO

**Fresh issue:** New shares are created and sold; proceeds go to the company
for business use. Increases equity share capital.

**Offer for sale (OFS):** Existing shareholders (promoters, investors) sell
their shares. No new capital comes to the company; money goes to sellers.

Most IPOs are a combination of both.

---

## How to Apply for an IPO

**Step 1 — Check the IPO calendar.** SEBI mandates a minimum 3-day subscription
window. IPO details are published in the red herring prospectus (RHP) on the
company's and exchanges' websites.

**Step 2 — Apply via ASBA.** Applications Supported by Blocked Amount (ASBA)
is the mandatory application method. Your application amount is blocked in your
bank account (not debited) until allotment. Apply through:
- Net banking of your bank
- UPI-based application via trading apps (Zerodha, Groww, Upstox)
- UPI mandate via BHIM/PhonePe/Google Pay

**Step 3 — Wait for allotment.** For oversubscribed IPOs, allotment is by
lottery for retail investors (each applying for 1 lot has equal probability).
Results announced within 6 days of issue close.

**Step 4 — Listing day.** Shares are credited to Demat account and trade from
listing date.

---

## Key Terms

**Price band:** The range within which investors can bid (e.g. ₹120–125). Most
apply at the cut-off price (highest end) to maximise allotment probability.

**Lot size:** The minimum number of shares per application (e.g. 150 shares).
Retail investors can apply for a maximum value of ₹2 lakh.

**GMP (Grey Market Premium):** An unofficial premium at which IPO shares trade
in unregulated grey markets before listing. It signals expected listing price
but is unreliable and speculative.

**Oversubscription:** When applications exceed shares available. A 50× subscribed
retail portion means only 1 in 50 applicants gets an allotment.

**Lock-up period:** Promoters and anchor investors are restricted from selling
their shares for a fixed period post-listing (usually 90–180 days).

---

## IPO Categories

| Category | Quota | Who applies |
|---|---|---|
| Retail Individual Investors (RII) | 35% of issue | Applications ≤ ₹2 lakh |
| Non-Institutional Investors (NII) | 15% of issue | Applications > ₹2 lakh |
| Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIB) | 50% of issue | Mutual funds, FIIs, banks |

Retail investors (RII) get 35% of shares; allotment by computer lottery for
oversubscribed issues.

## Evaluating an IPO

Unlike buying a listed stock (where years of price history and analyst coverage
exist), IPOs have:
- Prospectus but no market-tested price history
- Usually lower analyst coverage at launch
- Promoter and VC selling pressure if OFS-heavy

**Key questions to ask:**
1. **Why is the company raising money?** (Fresh issue for growth = positive;
   pure OFS where promoters cash out = less aligned)
2. **What are the financials?** Revenue growth, profit margins, debt levels
   (available in RHP)
3. **What is the valuation?** Compare P/E, P/S to listed peers in the same sector
4. **Who are the promoters?** Track record, background, prior businesses
5. **What are the risks?** Regulatory risk, competition, dependence on a few
   customers

---

## Listing Gains vs Long-Term Investing

**Listing gain strategy (IPO flipping):**
- Apply to sell on listing day for quick profit
- Works well for buzz-driven, oversubscribed IPOs in bull markets
- Risky: badly priced or poorly-received IPOs list at a discount

**Long-term holding:**
- More durable wealth creation
- Not all IPOs that list well create lasting value (e.g. many tech IPOs of 2021
  listed at premium but fell 50–70% in 2022)
- Requires genuine analysis of business quality

**Reality check:** Most retail investors apply to IPOs mainly for listing gains,
not long-term investing. For a first-time investor, the safer path is to let
the stock trade for 6–12 months post-listing, build an analyst track record,
and then buy at market price if fundamentals support it.

---

## Risks of IPO Investing

- **Overvaluation at listing price:** Companies and bankers set IPO prices
  to maximise proceeds; retail investors rarely get a "cheap" entry
- **Lock-up expiry selling pressure:** When promoter and VC lock-ups expire,
  significant selling can depress prices
- **Information asymmetry:** Promoters know the business far better than retail
  investors reading a prospectus
- **Hype-driven demand:** High GMP and subscription numbers don't guarantee
  listing gains

---

## SME IPOs

Smaller companies list on BSE SME or NSE Emerge platforms. Key differences:
- Minimum application: ₹1–1.5 lakh (higher than main board)
- Less regulatory scrutiny; higher risk
- Very illiquid post-listing; bid-ask spreads can be wide
- Not suitable for most retail investors unless familiar with the specific business

---

## Summary

- An IPO lets a private company raise money from the public for the first time.
- Apply via ASBA through banking or trading apps; amount is blocked, not debited.
- Retail investors are allotted by lottery in oversubscribed issues.
- Evaluate IPOs on fundamentals (revenue, margins, valuation vs peers), not GMP.
- Listing gains are possible but not guaranteed; long-term wealth requires
  holding quality businesses, not flipping.
- Many IPOs underperform in the year after listing — wait for a track record
  before committing large amounts.
