## Two different jobs

An **index fund** holds the constituents of an index — NIFTY 50, Sensex, NIFTY Next 50 — in the
same weights, aiming to match the index rather than beat it. Success is measured by *low tracking
error*, the gap between fund and index returns.

An **actively managed fund** employs a manager who selects securities aiming to outperform a
benchmark. Success is measured by *alpha*, return above the benchmark after costs.

## Why cost dominates the comparison

SEBI caps expense ratios, but the gap between categories is wide: index funds commonly charge
0.1% to 0.4%, active equity funds 0.5% to 1.25% for direct plans and up to around 2.25% for
regular plans.

That difference compounds. On ₹10 lakh growing at 12% gross over 20 years, a 0.2% expense ratio
leaves roughly ₹89 lakh, while 1.8% leaves roughly ₹65 lakh — about ₹24 lakh of difference
produced purely by cost, with no difference in skill assumed.

This is why the active fund must beat the index by more than the cost gap *consistently*, not
occasionally, to be the better choice.

## The Indian evidence, honestly stated

In large-cap, a majority of active funds have struggled to beat their benchmarks over multi-year
periods, which SEBI's mandatory benchmarking disclosures made visible. Two things made this
sharper: the 2018 recategorisation restricted what a large-cap fund may hold, and the move to
Total Return Index benchmarks removed a dividend-shaped advantage in the comparison.

In mid-cap and small-cap the picture is less settled — less efficient price discovery leaves more
room for selection to matter, and a greater share of active funds there have beaten their
benchmarks over some periods. Past outperformance of any specific fund is not evidence it
continues.

## What actually separates them

- **Tracking error** is the index fund's quality metric, not returns.
- **Alpha and downside capture** are the active fund's metrics.
- **ETF versus index fund**: an ETF trades on exchange and needs a demat account, and may trade
  at a premium or discount to NAV; an index fund transacts at NAV with no demat requirement.
