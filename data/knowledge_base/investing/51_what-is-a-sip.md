## The mechanism

A Systematic Investment Plan is an instruction to invest a fixed amount in a chosen mutual fund
scheme at a fixed interval, usually monthly, via auto-debit from your bank account. On each
date, that amount buys units at the prevailing net asset value.

The crucial point beginners miss: **a SIP is not an asset class or a product.** It is a payment
method. "I invested in a SIP" is as imprecise as "I invested in a bank transfer" — the outcome
depends entirely on the underlying scheme. The same SIP route can feed a liquid fund, an index
fund, or a small-cap fund, with wildly different risk.

## Rupee cost averaging

Because the amount is fixed, a falling NAV buys more units and a rising NAV buys fewer. Over a
volatile period this produces an average cost lower than the average NAV — the arithmetic works
out that way whenever prices fluctuate.

A worked illustration with ₹5,000 monthly:

| Month | NAV | Units bought |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹50 | 100.0 |
| 2 | ₹40 | 125.0 |
| 3 | ₹25 | 200.0 |
| 4 | ₹50 | 100.0 |

Total invested ₹20,000, total units 525, average cost ₹38.10 — against an average NAV of ₹41.25.

## What rupee cost averaging does not do

It does not prevent losses. If the market is lower when you need the money than your average
cost, you lose money. It does not beat a lump sum in a steadily rising market, where investing
everything early would have bought more. What it reliably does is remove the need to time entry,
and spread the regret of any single bad entry date across many dates.

## Practical mechanics in India

The minimum is commonly ₹500 a month, sometimes ₹100. A SIP can be paused, modified, or stopped
without penalty — the fund cannot force continued contributions. **Step-up** or top-up SIPs
raise the amount by a set percentage annually, which tracks salary growth. ELSS schemes are the
exception to free exit: each instalment carries its own three-year lock-in.
