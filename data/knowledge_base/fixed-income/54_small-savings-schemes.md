## What they have in common

Small savings schemes are sovereign-backed deposit products distributed through post offices and
designated banks. Rates are notified by the Ministry of Finance and reviewed quarterly. Credit
risk is effectively nil; the trade-offs are lock-ins, deposit caps, and rates that government
can reset.

## The main schemes

**National Savings Certificate (NSC)** — five-year term, interest compounded annually but paid
at maturity. Deposits qualify under Section 80C in the old regime. The accrued interest of years
one to four is itself treated as reinvested, so it can also be claimed under 80C in those years.

**Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana (SSY)** — for a girl child below ten. Deposits run for 15 years, the
account matures at 21 years from opening, and up to 50% can be withdrawn after she turns 18 for
higher education. It typically carries the highest rate among small savings schemes and is EEE.
Annual deposits range from ₹250 to ₹1.5 lakh.

**Senior Citizens' Savings Scheme (SCSS)** — for those aged 60 and above, five-year term
extendable by three, with interest paid quarterly. The deposit limit was raised to ₹30 lakh.
Interest is taxable at slab rate.

**Kisan Vikas Patra (KVP)** — doubles the deposit over a notified period, currently around nine
years and change. No 80C deduction, and interest is taxable.

**Monthly Income Scheme (POMIS)** — five-year term paying monthly interest, useful where regular
cash flow matters more than growth.

## Where they fit

These are debt instruments, not growth instruments. Their place is the fixed-income portion of a
goal where capital safety outweighs return, or where a specific feature matches a specific need —
SSY for a daughter's education timeline, SCSS for a retiree needing quarterly income.

For long-horizon goals the main limitation is that returns tend to track inflation rather than
beat it meaningfully, which is the argument for pairing them with equity exposure in a
multi-decade plan rather than relying on them alone.

*Educational information only. Rates are revised quarterly and limits change — verify current
terms with the National Savings Institute or a post office.*
