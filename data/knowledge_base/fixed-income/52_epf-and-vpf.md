## How the contribution splits

For most salaried employees in India, 12% of basic salary plus dearness allowance goes to the
Employees' Provident Fund, and the employer contributes a matching 12%. The employer's share is
not all EPF, though — 8.33% of it is diverted to the Employees' Pension Scheme (EPS), subject to
a wage ceiling, with the remaining 3.67% going to EPF.

The EPF interest rate is declared annually by EPFO and has recently sat in the 8.1% to 8.25%
band, typically the highest available on a sovereign-backed fixed-income product.

## VPF: the voluntary top-up

An employee can contribute more than the mandatory 12% through the Voluntary Provident Fund, up
to 100% of basic plus DA. VPF earns the same rate as EPF and the employer is not required to
match it. There is one important tax boundary: interest on employee contributions exceeding
₹2.5 lakh in a financial year is taxable, so very large VPF contributions lose part of the
advantage.

## Tax treatment

EPF is EEE for most employees, provided the account completes five years of continuous service.
Withdrawals before five years are taxable, and the earlier 80C deductions can be reversed. The
employee's own contribution qualifies under 80C in the old regime; the employer's contribution
is not counted against that limit, but employer contributions above ₹7.5 lakh a year across
EPF, NPS, and superannuation are taxable as a perquisite.

## What to actually do with it

Two practical points matter more than the rate. First, **transfer rather than withdraw** when
changing jobs: using the UAN to transfer preserves the five-year continuity and the compounding.
Withdrawing to fund a purchase resets both. Second, the EPS pension is modest because it is
calculated on a capped pensionable salary, so it is usually a supplement rather than a
retirement plan.

Balance and passbook are viewable through the EPFO member portal using the UAN.
