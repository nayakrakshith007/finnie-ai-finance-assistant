## The bundled products

Endowment plans, money-back policies, and ULIPs (unit-linked insurance plans) combine life cover
with a savings or market-linked component. They are sold heavily in India, often around
January to March when buyers are looking for tax deductions.

The structural problem is that one premium is being asked to do two jobs. The protection part is
small — a ₹50,000 annual endowment premium often buys only ₹5 lakh to ₹10 lakh of cover, which
would not replace a salary. The savings part carries the policy's commissions and administration
charges, so returns on traditional endowment plans have historically landed in the 4% to 6%
range, before accounting for inflation.

## The unbundled comparison

Separating the two jobs is usually cheaper and clearer:

| | Bundled endowment | Term + index fund |
|---|---|---|
| ₹50,000/year buys | ~₹5–10 lakh cover, ~4–6% returns | ₹1 crore+ cover (~₹12,000) and ~₹38,000 invested |
| Charges | Opaque, front-loaded | Expense ratio, visible |
| Exit before maturity | Surrender value well below premiums paid | Sell units at market value |
| What you can see | A single maturity projection | Cover amount and fund NAV, separately |

## Where ULIPs differ

ULIPs are more transparent than traditional endowment plans — you can see the fund NAV — but
they carry a five-year lock-in, premium allocation and policy administration charges, and
mortality charges deducted by cancelling units. They are not simply mutual funds with a life
cover attached.

## The honest exception

If a policy is already several years old, surrendering it is not automatically the better
outcome, because surrender charges and the lost cover have to be weighed against what the
remaining premiums could do elsewhere. That calculation depends on the specific policy's
surrender value, paid-up option, and remaining term.

*Educational information only. This explains product structures, not what any individual should
